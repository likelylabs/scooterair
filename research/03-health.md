## How to read the confidence tags

- **VERIFIED** = I retrieved the document or record itself in this session (WHO/IARC/HEI/MOHW PDFs or pages; PubMed efetch records; Crossref/OpenAlex records that reproduce a paper's abstract) and saw the quoted text. Verbatim quotes are copied from what I retrieved. "VERIFIED (abstract)" means I saw the abstract but not the full text.
- **SECONDARY** = reported by a reputable secondary source or a search-tool summary, not opened by me.
- **UNVERIFIED** = background knowledge or not located. Do not publish.

## 3. Taiwan-specific health evidence

### 3a. Childhood asthma and lung function

1. **Guo C, Hoek G, Chang LY, Bo Y, Lin C, Huang B, Chan TC, Tam T, Lau AKH, Lao XQ. 2019.** "Long-Term Exposure to Ambient Fine Particulate Matter (PM2.5) and Lung Function in Children, Adolescents, and Young Adults: A Longitudinal Cohort Study." *Environmental Health Perspectives* 127(12). doi 10.1289/EHP5220 (Crossref author/title record and OpenAlex/Crossref abstract opened). **VERIFIED (abstract).**
   - Design: 24,544 participants aged 6-24 with 33,506 medical observations 2000-2014 in Taiwan; spatiotemporal model for home-address PM2.5; generalized linear mixed model.
   - Quote: "Every 10-ug/m3 increase in the 2-y average PM2.5 concentration was associated with decreases of 2.22% [95% CI: -2.60, -1.85], 2.94 (95% CI: -3.36, -2.51), and 2.79% (95% CI: -3.15, -2.41) in the FVC, FEV1, and MMEF, respectively. Furthermore, it was associated with a 20% increase in the prevalence of poor lung function (OR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.12, 1.29)."
   - Caveats: ambient (all-source) PM2.5, not scooter-specific; observational. Whether this is the "Taiwan Children Health Study" cohort is not stated in the abstract; do not call it TCHS.
2. **Tsai YG, Chio CP, Yang KD, Lin CH, Yeh YP, Chang YJ, Chien JW, Wang SL, Huang SK, Chan CC. 2025.** "Long-term PM2.5 exposure is associated with asthma prevalence and exhaled nitric oxide levels in children." *Pediatric Research* 97(1):370-377 (epub Jan 2024). doi 10.1038/s41390-023-02977-5; PMID 38263452 (PubMed record opened). **VERIFIED (abstract).**
   - Design: 4,736 school children in six townships near industrial areas (coal-fired power plants) in central Taiwan (Changhua); land-use regression for PM2.5, NO, SO2.
   - Quote: "Annual exposure to PM2.5 was associated with increased odds of physician-diagnosed asthma (OR = 1.595), exercise-induced wheezing (OR = 1.726)..." and "An increase of 1 ug/m3 PM2.5 exposure was significantly associated with a 1.0% increase in FENO levels ... after adjusting for ... NO and SO2." Abstract also lists FENO correlated with being "near the road".
   - Caveats: cross-sectional; industrial/coal setting, not scooter-specific; the OR is per an unstated increment in the abstract, so do not quote it as "per 1 ug/m3".
3. **Hwang BF, Lee YL, Lin YC, Jaakkola JJ, Guo YL. 2005.** "Traffic related air pollution as a determinant of asthma among Taiwanese school children." *Thorax* 60(6):467-73. doi 10.1136/thx.2004.033977; PMID 15923246 (record opened). **VERIFIED (abstract).** Nationwide cross-sectional study of 32,672 children in 2001 using routine monitoring data. Results: asthma positively associated with O3 (adjusted OR 1.138, 95% CI 1.001-1.293 per 10 ppb), CO (OR 1.045, 1.017-1.074 per 100 ppb), NOx (OR 1.005, 0.954-1.117, not significant); PM10 (OR 0.934, 0.909-0.960) was inversely associated. Authors' conclusion: "consistent with the hypothesis that long term exposure to traffic related outdoor air pollutants such as NOx, CO, and O3 increases the risk of asthma in children." Caveat: the conclusion is stronger than the NOx estimate (non-significant); monitoring-station data; cross-sectional; 2000-2001 exposure levels. Cite with the actual numbers, not the headline.
4. **Cho C-I, Chen J-J, Chuang K-J, Chuang H-C, Wang I-J, Chang T-Y. 2023.** "Associations of particulate matter, gaseous pollutants, and road traffic noise with the prevalence of asthma in children." *Chemosphere* 338:139523. doi 10.1016/j.chemosphere.2023.139523; PMID 37459931 (record opened). **VERIFIED (abstract).** 3,246 children from 11 kindergartens in New Taipei City; land use regression. Quote: "an interquartile-range (IQR) increase in PM2.5 (1.17 ug/m3) and PM10 (10.69 ug/m3) caused a 1.34-fold (95% CI = 1.05-1.70) and 1.17-fold (95% CI = 1.01-1.36) increase in risk of asthma prevalence in children after adjusting for LAeq,24 and NO2." NO2 and road traffic noise "did not demonstrate significant associations". Caveat: cross-sectional ("prevalence"); "caused" in the abstract is loose wording.
5. **Lin T-Y et al. 2024.** "Different Impacts of Traffic-Related Air Pollution on Early-Onset and Late-Onset Asthma." *J Asthma Allergy* 17:195-208, doi 10.2147/JAA.S451725 (PMID 38505396; abstract opened). 675 asthma patients, Taiwan medical centre; PM10, PM2.5 and NO2 associated with lower lung function in early-onset asthma patients. Adult/clinic patients, cross-sectional. **VERIFIED (abstract).**
6. Taiwan Children Health Study (TCHS) traffic-specific papers: **not located** with a verifiable abstract this pass. Do not cite TCHS for traffic effects until a DOI is verified.

### 3c. Lung cancer in never-smokers (what the evidence does and does not say)

- **Tseng CH, Tsuang BJ, Chiang CJ, Ku KC, Tseng JS, Yang TY, Hsu KH, Chen KC, Yu SL, Lee WC, Liu TW, Chan CC, Chang GC. 2019.** "The Relationship Between Air Pollution and Lung Cancer in Nonsmokers in Taiwan." *J Thorac Oncol* 14(5):784-792. doi 10.1016/j.jtho.2018.12.033; PMID 30664991 (record opened). **VERIFIED (abstract).** Ecological/registry analysis: 371,084 lung cancer patients; Northern vs Southern Taiwan. Quotes: "From 1995 to 2015, the proportion of male adult ever-smokers decreased from 59.4% to 29.9% whereas the female smoking rate remained low (3.2% to 5.3%). AdLC incidence in males and females increased from 9.06 to 23.25 and 7.05 to 24.22 per 100,000 population, respectively."; "53% patients with LC had never smoked."; "In Taiwan, greater than 50% of patients with LC had never smoked. PM2.5 level changes can affect AdLC incidence and patient survival." Caveats: ecological regional comparison using visibility as an air-quality proxy; cannot show that PM2.5 caused individual cancers; the 5-year survival numbers (12.6% north vs 4.5% south, HR 0.79) are for a specific subset.
- **Hill W, Lim EL, Weeden CE, et al. 2023.** "Lung adenocarcinoma promotion by air pollutants." *Nature* 616(7955):159-167. doi 10.1038/s41586-023-05874-3; PMID 37020004; PMC7614604 (PubMed abstract and Europe PMC full text opened). **VERIFIED.** Abstract: "we found a significant association between PM2.5 levels and the incidence of lung cancer for 32,957 EGFR-driven lung cancer cases in four within-country cohorts. Functional mouse models revealed that air pollutants cause an influx of macrophages into the lung and release of interleukin-1beta." Full text (Taiwan part): England, South Korea and Taiwan country-level analyses; "The relative rates of EGFR-driven lung cancer incidence (per 100,000 population), per 1 ug m-3 increment of PM2.5 levels were 0.63 (P = 0.0028) in England, 0.71 (P = 0.0091) in South Korea and 1.82 (P = 4.01 x 10-6) in Taiwan." The Taiwan data came from the Chang Gung Research Database, 2011-2017, nine counties, with PM2.5 averaged by county. Caveats: the human-cohort analyses are ecological (area-level) and the paper itself notes it "was not able to account for the migration of individuals"; the causal mechanism evidence is from mouse models and small tissue samples; PM2.5 is all-source, not scooter-specific.
- Honest summary: IARC has classified outdoor air pollution and PM as causing lung cancer in general (Group 1). Evidence that air pollution contributes to the Taiwanese excess of EGFR-mutant adenocarcinoma in never-smokers is suggestive (ecological associations plus mouse mechanism), not established as a cause for individual patients, and other factors are not excluded by these studies.

## 4. Taiwanese commuter panel studies (scooter / walking)

**Chuang K-J, Lin L-Y, Ho K-F, Su C-T. 2020.** "Traffic-related PM2.5 exposure and its cardiovascular effects among healthy commuters in Taipei, Taiwan." *Atmospheric Environment: X* 7:100084 (Oct 2020). doi 10.1016/j.aeaoa.2020.100084 (Crossref metadata opened; abstract text from OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar APIs, identical). Open-access (gold). **VERIFIED (abstract).** Full-text not read, so the per-increment basis of the percentages is not confirmed.
- Design: "We recruited 120 healthy adults in Taipei, Taiwan. Each participant was classified by his/her own commuting style, including electrically powered subway, gasoline-powered bus, gasoline-powered car, gasoline-powered scooter, walking, and walking wearing a facemask. Six repeated measurements of systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic BP (DBP), heart rate (HR), urinary 8-hydroxy-2'-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), ... PM2.5, temperature, humidity and noise level were conducted ... between 09:00 am and 10:00 am". Mixed-effects models.
- Key quotes: "personal exposure to PM2.5 was associated with increased SBP (W: 6.5%; Sc: 7.9%), HR (W: 3.4%; Sc: 6.4%) and urinary 8-OHdG (W: 8.1%; Sc: 8.9%) in the walking (W) mode and scooter (Sc) mode. The PM2.5 level (Mean +/- SD) was the highest in the scooter mode (53.6 +/- 26.1 ug/m3) but the lowest in the subway mode (21.7 +/- 5.8 ug/m3). The effects of PM2.5 on cardiovascular endpoints were not statistically significant in the subway mode. Additionally, no significant association was noted among PM2.5, BP and HR in the walking wearing a facemask mode."
- Caveats: the abstract does not state the PM2.5 increment the percentages are expressed per, so do not publish the percentages as a standalone effect size; healthy adults; one hour of morning commute; 8-OHdG is an oxidative DNA damage marker, not a disease; the scooter PM2.5 includes the rider's own immersion in traffic; no scooter-specific source apportionment; the authors' conclusion ("traffic-related PM2.5 was associated with cardiovascular effects") is association language.
- The earlier "2015 study of 120 commuters" I listed in the first pass came from a search summary and appears to be the same publication; I could not confirm a separate 2015 paper, so ignore it. Co-authors "Chan CC, Liu WT, Wu CF" are not on this paper.

**Wang C-Y, Lim B-S, Wang Y-H, Huang Y-CT. 2021.** "Identification of High Personal PM2.5 Exposure during Real Time Commuting in the Taipei Metropolitan Area." *Atmosphere* 12(3):396. doi 10.3390/atmos12030396 (OpenAlex abstract opened). **VERIFIED (abstract).** Quotes: "Exposure to PM2.5 (median (range)) was higher when walking along the streets (40 (10-275) ug/m3) compared to riding the buses (35 (13-65) ug/m3) and the cars (15 (8-80) ug/m3)"; "Riding motorcycle also was associated with high PM2.5 exposure (75 (60-105 ug/m3), p < 0.0001 vs. walking)." ## 6. Near-road gradients and health: HEI

**HEI Panel on the Health Effects of Long-Term Exposure to Traffic-Related Air Pollution. *Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Selected Health Effects of Long-Term Exposure to Traffic-Related Air Pollution.* HEI Special Report 23. Boston: Health Effects Institute; June 2022 (updated October 14, 2022).** Executive Summary PDF https://www.healtheffects.org/system/files/hei-special-report-23-executive-summary_1.pdf (opened). **VERIFIED (executive summary; the full report was not opened).**

- Scope: "In total, 353 studies were included in the review." Studies by region: "Europe (N = 163 studies, 46%), and North America (N = 130 studies, 37%). Studies in Asia (predominantly China) emerged more recently (N = 41 studies, 12%)." Panel of 13 experts.
- Key conclusion quote: "The Panel found a high or moderate-to-high level of confidence in an association between long-term exposure to TRAP and the adverse health outcomes all-cause, circulatory, ischemic heart disease (IHD), and lung cancer mortality; asthma onset in both children and adults; and acute lower respiratory infections (ALRI) in children. The Panel's confidence in the evidence was considered moderate, low, or very low for the other selected outcomes."

## 7. Short-term exposure: what is and is not established

- **Brook RD, Rajagopalan S, Pope CA 3rd, et al.; AHA Council on Epidemiology and Prevention. 2010.** "Particulate matter air pollution and cardiovascular disease: An update to the scientific statement from the American Heart Association." *Circulation* 121(21):2331-78. doi 10.1161/CIR.0b013e3181dbece1; PMID 20458016 (record opened). **VERIFIED (abstract).** Quotes: "Exposure to PM <2.5 microm in diameter (PM2.5) over a few hours to weeks can trigger cardiovascular disease-related mortality and nonfatal events; longer-term exposure (eg, a few years) increases the risk for cardiovascular mortality to an even greater extent than exposures over a few days". "It is the opinion of the writing group that the overall evidence is consistent with a causal relationship between PM2.5 exposure and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality." Caveat: US ambient PM2.5, not specific to scooter exhaust or to minutes-long peaks; "hours to weeks" is the shortest window the statement addresses.
- Chuang et al. 2020 (section 4): within about one commute hour, higher personal PM2.5 was associated with higher blood pressure, heart rate and a urinary oxidative-damage marker in healthy adults. This is an association with small, transient physiological changes, not evidence of illness.
- WHO's 2021 24-hour and 8-hour values are short-term guidelines ("Annual and peak season is long-term exposure, while 24 hour and 8 hour is short-term exposure", WHO Q&A; VERIFIED).
- **Not established / not found:** I found no source showing health effects from a few minutes behind an idling scooter, nor any threshold for such exposures. The sources above address hours to weeks (Brook) or an hour-long commute (Chuang). The site should say that peak exposures near traffic are measured to be higher, but that the health consequences of individual brief encounters are not established.

## (a) Corrections to the handover

1. "Gasoline engine exhaust is IARC Group 2B": confirmed (IARC press release 213: "Group 2B ... unchanged from the previous evaluation in 1989"). Diesel is Group 1 (Vol. 105, 2012) and must not be conflated.
2. IARC volumes: Vol. 105 (2012 meeting; diesel/gasoline exhaust), Vol. 109 (2013 meeting; outdoor air pollution/PM), Vol. 120 (2017 meeting, published 2018; benzene). The press-release/journal years are verified; the publication years of the Vol. 105 (2014?) and Vol. 109 (2016?) books are not verified.
3. WHO 2021 values are verified: PM2.5 5 annual / 15 24-h; PM10 15 / 45; O3 60 peak season / 100 8-h; NO2 10 annual / 25 24-h; SO2 40 24-h; CO 4 mg/m3 24-h. "7 million deaths" is the combined ambient + household figure, not ambient alone (ambient alone: "between 4 million and 9 million" in the same document).
4. "PM2.5 from traffic linked to cardiovascular effects (blood pressure, heart rate, oxidative stress markers) in scooter and walking modes": **supported** by Chuang et al. 2020 (SBP, HR, urinary 8-OHdG; scooter and walking). Authors are Chuang, Lin, Ho, Su; the "Chan CC, Liu WT, Wu CF" attribution is not correct for this paper. Say "associated with", healthy adults, Taipei, one commute hour.
5. Lung cancer is the leading cancer cause of death in Taiwan (46.4 per 100,000 in 2025 data), not the leading cause of death overall (cancer overall is first, 44 consecutive years).
6. HEI SR23's confidence statements are about *association*, "high or moderate-to-high" for asthma onset, ALRI, and mortality outcomes; the Panel states it did not assess causality. SR23 does not list lung function. HEI SR17 (2010) judged *exacerbation* of asthma in children "sufficient" for causality, and childhood asthma onset only "suggestive".
7. The "44% higher at pram height" figure is real, but it is "up to 44%" for fine particles, from a UK study, for in-pram height vs adult breathing height. WHO says "children live closer to the ground, where some pollutants reach peak concentrations."
8. Hill et al. 2023 (Nature) does include Taiwanese data (Chang Gung Research Database), but it is an area-level ecological analysis plus mouse work.
9. Poll claim: only a non-random online poll found; leave unverified.

## (b) Safe-to-publish claims

Each is calibrated to the evidence retrieved; wording in plain language. Tags give source and confidence.

1. Outdoor air pollution, and the fine particles in it, are classified by the WHO's cancer agency (IARC) as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1), because they cause lung cancer. [IARC press release 221, 2013, Monographs Vol. 109; VERIFIED]
2. Benzene, a component of petrol (gasoline) and of petrol exhaust, is classified by IARC as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) and causes acute myeloid leukaemia. [IARC Monographs Vol. 120, 2018; VERIFIED]
3. IARC classifies gasoline engine exhaust as "possibly carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2B); diesel engine exhaust is Group 1. [IARC press release 213, 2012; VERIFIED]
4. WHO's 2021 guideline for PM2.5 is 5 ug/m3 as a yearly average and 15 ug/m3 as a 24-hour average. [WHO 2021 Global AQG, Table 0.1; VERIFIED]
5. WHO's 2021 guideline for NO2 is 10 ug/m3 as a yearly average and 25 ug/m3 as a 24-hour average; its 24-hour guideline for carbon monoxide is 4 mg/m3. [WHO 2021 Global AQG, Table 0.1; VERIFIED]
6. WHO estimates around 7 million deaths a year are attributable to the combined effects of outdoor and household air pollution. [WHO 2021 Global AQG, section 1.3.3; VERIFIED]
7. WHO says fine particles (PM2.5) can enter the bloodstream and mainly affect the heart and lungs. [WHO Q&A on the 2021 guidelines; VERIFIED]
8. A 2022 review by the Health Effects Institute of 353 studies found high or moderate-to-high confidence that long-term exposure to traffic-related air pollution is associated with asthma onset in children and adults, acute lower respiratory infections in children, and deaths from heart disease and lung cancer. [HEI Special Report 23 executive summary; VERIFIED]
9. In Taipei, healthy adults commuting by scooter had average personal PM2.5 exposure of 53.6 ug/m3, compared with 21.7 ug/m3 on the subway. [Chuang et al. 2020, Atmos Environ X; VERIFIED (abstract)]
10. In the same Taipei study, higher personal PM2.5 was associated with higher blood pressure, heart rate and a urinary marker of oxidative DNA damage when walking or riding a scooter, but not in the subway. [Chuang et al. 2020; VERIFIED (abstract)]
11. A study of more than 24,000 Taiwanese children and young adults found that higher two-year average PM2.5 was associated with lower lung function (about 2-3% lower for each 10 ug/m3). [Guo et al. 2019, EHP; VERIFIED (abstract)]
12. In a Taiwanese study of 4,736 schoolchildren near industrial areas, higher long-term PM2.5 exposure was associated with more physician-diagnosed asthma and with higher exhaled nitric oxide, a marker of airway inflammation. [Tsai et al. 2025, Pediatr Res; VERIFIED (abstract); note industrial setting]
13. WHO says children breathe faster than adults, taking in more air and more pollutants, and live closer to the ground, where some pollutants reach peak concentrations. [WHO 2018, Air pollution and child health (Summary); VERIFIED]
14. In a UK experiment (not in Taiwan), fine-particle concentrations at baby-in-pram height were up to 44% higher than at adult breathing height. [Sharma and Kumar 2020, Environ Int; VERIFIED (abstract)]
15. An American Thoracic Society workshop concluded that long-term exposure to air pollution is a cause of childhood asthma, while the evidence for adult asthma or COPD is insufficient. [Thurston et al. 2020, Ann Am Thorac Soc; VERIFIED (abstract)]
16. Lung cancer is the leading cancer cause of death in Taiwan; in 2025 the lung cancer death rate was 46.4 per 100,000 people. [MOHW 114年死因統計結果分析; VERIFIED]
17. More than half of lung cancer patients in Taiwan have never smoked (53% in one analysis of 371,084 patients), and researchers are investigating whether air pollution contributes. [Tseng et al. 2019, J Thorac Oncol; VERIFIED (abstract)]
18. A modelled estimate for 2014 attributed about 6,300 Taiwanese deaths from heart disease, stroke, lung cancer and COPD to ambient PM2.5. [Lo et al. 2017, J Formos Med Assoc; VERIFIED (abstract); label as dated and modelled]
19. WHO's European guidelines recommend keeping road traffic noise below 53 dB Lden (average) and 45 dB at night. [WHO Europe 2018; VERIFIED; European guideline, not scooter-specific]
20. The American Heart Association concluded that PM2.5 exposure over a few hours to weeks can trigger cardiovascular events, and that overall evidence is consistent with a causal relationship with cardiovascular illness. [Brook et al. 2010, Circulation; VERIFIED (abstract); general ambient PM2.5]

## (c) Do not claim

- That gasoline scooter exhaust is IARC Group 1, or "as carcinogenic as diesel".
- That scooter exhaust "causes" a specific person's asthma, cancer or heart disease; or that minutes behind an idling scooter cause illness (no source shows this).
- That the Taiwanese studies isolate scooters. They measure ambient PM2.5, traffic-related pollution or commute-mode exposure; none attributes health effects specifically to scooter exhaust.
- That air pollution is "the cause" of Taiwanese never-smokers' lung cancer, or any specific percentage of it. Tseng 2019 and Hill 2023 are ecological or mechanistic.
- Any specific effect size from Chuang 2020 as "per 1 ug/m3" (the abstract does not state the increment); and not the 8-OHdG as "proof of DNA damage causing disease".
- That children are exposed "44% more" than adults in general, or any Taiwanese child-height measurement (none found). The 2018 "60% more at pram height" figure is unverified.
- That WHO's "7 million deaths" are all from outdoor air pollution (it combines household and ambient).
- Taiwan-specific figures for PM2.5-attributable deaths later than 2014, or any figure for deaths "from scooters". None retrieved.
- Taiwanese dementia numbers from Jung et al. 2015 (implausible reported magnitudes).
- That Hwang 2005 shows NOx causes asthma in Taiwanese children (its NOx estimate was not significant, and PM10 was inversely associated).
- That a majority of Taiwanese support stricter controls because of health fears (no representative poll found).
- That HEI SR23 proves causation or covers lung function.
