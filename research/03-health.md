### 1b. Component-by-component (hazard descriptions)

## 4. Taiwan commuter panel studies (scooter / walking)

**Chuang KJ, Lin LY, Ho HC, Su HJ et al. (author list per search summary: "Chuang, Lin, Ho, Su") 2020.** "Traffic-related PM2.5 exposure and its cardiovascular effects among healthy commuters in Taipei, Taiwan." *Atmospheric Environment: X* 7: 100084. DOI 10.1016/j.aeaoa.2020.100084 (DOI per search; page https://hub.tmu.edu.tw/en/publications/traffic-related-pmsub25sub-exposure-and-its-cardiovascular-effect/ ; unopened).
- Design (per summary): repeated-measures panel of healthy commuters in Taipei across commuting modes: subway, scooter, walking, and walking with face mask. Personal PM2.5 measured; heart rate and blood pressure measured. Sample size not confirmed.
- Findings (SECONDARY): mean personal PM2.5 53.6 +/- 26.1 ug/m3 in scooter mode vs 21.7 +/- 5.8 ug/m3 in subway mode. PM2.5 associated with higher systolic blood pressure and heart rate in walking and scooter modes; one summary says heart rate rose 3.4% (walking) and 6.4% (scooter) (the increment of PM2.5 for those percentages is not stated - do not publish without it). Not significant in subway mode; no significant association in walkers wearing masks.
- Caveats: small panel of healthy young adults; short-term changes of unclear clinical meaning; the scooter-mode PM2.5 includes ambient and traffic contributions, plus in-traffic exposure of riders themselves (which relates to Q8). Oxidative stress markers were NOT reported in this paper per the summary.

**Earlier Chuang group study, 2015** (PubMed 25638696; https://www.pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25638696/ ; title per TMU hub: "Traffic-related PM2.5 exposure and its cardiovascular effects among healthy commuters in Taipei, Taiwan" appears to be shared across two entries; I could not tell which is which, so the exact title/journal/year of the 2015 paper is UNVERIFIED). Summary: 120 young healthy commuters; HRV decreased as PM2.5 rose; highest personal PM2.5 while walking; subway smallest effects; modes included subway, gasoline bus, gasoline car, walking. SECONDARY. I could not confirm the claimed "Chan CC, Liu WT, Wu CF" involvement; the search summary said those co-authors did not appear. Related Chuang studies on in-car particles and oxidative stress (e.g., "Personal exposure to fine particles, inflammation and oxidative stress" TMU hub link) exist but I did not retrieve numbers.

Conclusion for the handover claim: "PM2.5 from traffic linked to cardiovascular effects (blood pressure, heart rate, oxidative stress markers) in scooter and walking modes" is **partly supported** (blood pressure and heart rate; scooter and walking) via Chuang 2020. The **oxidative stress marker** part is not supported by what I found for that paper.

## 7. Short-term (minutes to hours) exposure

I did not find a good primary source in this pass. What the sources I have support:
- Chuang 2020 (Q4): within a commute, higher personal PM2.5 was associated with higher heart rate and blood pressure in healthy adults. That is a measurable physiological response, not evidence of illness. SECONDARY.
- Established elsewhere but UNVERIFIED here: short-term PM2.5 elevations are associated with cardiovascular and respiratory events at the population level (time-series studies); brief peaks at intersections have not been shown to cause lasting harm in healthy people. ## (c) Do not claim

- That gasoline scooter exhaust is "Group 1" or "as carcinogenic as diesel."
- That scooter exhaust "causes" asthma, lung cancer or heart disease in a particular person; or that a few minutes behind an idling scooter causes illness.
- That air pollution is the cause of lung cancer in Taiwanese never-smoking women (evidence suggestive only).
