# When you can smell the fumes, what is in the air?

Estimate (推估), not a measurement. 2026-11-27. Method: `METHOD-SMELL.md`. Corrections: `corrections.md`.

## The direct answer

**When the smell can be detected.** If the smell you notice is the exhaust of the idling scooters beside you, the
same air also holds benzene, 1,3-butadiene and formaldehyde. Benzene, 1,3-butadiene and formaldehyde are three
chemicals that the World Health Organization's cancer agency (IARC) labels "causes cancer in humans". The air also
holds toluene, xylenes and about ten other exhaust chemicals.

**How much benzene.** At the moment the smell is just noticeable, benzene is typically about **2 to 15 parts per
billion (ppb)**. The two numbers come from two old smell tests. The full range is about 0.4 to 60 ppb. When the
wind blows from a group of idling scooters toward you, at a child's height half a metre from the nearest one, it is
about **30 ppb** over the seconds you can smell it (range about 2 to 360 ppb). In that wind you can smell it for
most of the red.

**What it means and does not mean.** Those cancer labels say how strong the evidence is that a chemical *can*
cause cancer. They are not the risk from a red light, and no such risk is worked out here. IARC labels petrol-engine
exhaust as a whole Group 2B, "possibly causes cancer". For seconds-long levels
there is no official value to compare with. One red light on its own does not bring any of these chemicals up to a
30-minute or 1-hour public reference level. For someone who stays at the kerb through every red for an hour, with
the wind from the scooters the whole time, benzene added by the scooters is above California's 1-hour reference
level. The signed simulation report already found this.

**Two things that cut the other way.**
- **What you smell is probably not benzene.** Benzene stays far below the level at which a nose can pick it up.
  Aldehydes, which are burnt-fuel products, are the likelier smell. Unburnt petrol vapour was not modelled. So
  "you smell petrol, and petrol causes cancer" is not supported.
- **No smell does not mean clean air.** Benzene is there in reds where nothing can be smelled. Carbon monoxide has
  no smell, and particles are too small to see.

## What is NOT known

- **Modern scooters.** Every share comes from old carburetted or uncatalysed engines (Taiwan 2008, India, Nepal)
  or from cars. For today's fuel-injected, catalysed Taiwanese scooters the mix is unknown, and the smell may be
  rarer.
- **Not estimated:** benzo[a]pyrene and other soot-bound chemicals (no usable data, and they have no smell),
  phenol (sources disagree 100 times), hydrogen sulphide (never measured in what we could open), dioxins.
- **The real street.** We have no figure for what is already in the street air, and the smell may come from other
  traffic or other sources.
- **Which chemical you actually smell** is only order-of-magnitude.
- **Two-stroke scooters** may carry more benzene than assumed.

## How sure

Low. Each case is one simulated red. Seconds near the scooters are limited by the grid. The wind is held where
stated. The smell threshold comes from two car-exhaust tests from 1967-70 that differ 8.5 times. The chemical
shares vary 2-20 times between sources: benzene's share has moderate confidence; acrolein and naphthalene are very
low. Every number above has a wide range and must be shown with it. A rare near-calm case (half a metre downwind of
one tailpipe, about 1 % of reds or fewer, likely to read high) gives much higher values. It is kept in the detail
tables only.
